Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning

A real-world application analysis

One machine-learning problem, one deep-learning problem — and a look at why each approach actually fits its problem, and why swapping them would fail. The real skill isn't knowing the algorithms; it's matching the tool to the shape of the problem.

Machine Learning A straight line through structured data — transparent and easy to explain.
vs

The deciding lens

Six questions that pick the tool

Before either case, here's the framework the whole analysis turns on. The choice between machine learning and deep learning isn't about which is more advanced — it's about the shape of the problem in front of you.

Case 01 · Machine Learning

Stock Beta Estimation

Method — Linear Regression
The real-world application

In finance, beta estimation is a core part of the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) that analysts and portfolio managers use every day. You run a simple linear regression of a stock's historical returns against a benchmark like the S&P 500 over the same period — the slope of that line is the beta, telling you how sensitive the stock is to overall market movement. This isn't theoretical: brokerages, robo-advisors, and trading platforms compute beta this way to help investors understand risk before they put money into a position.

CAPMPortfolio riskRobo-advisorsTrading platforms
✓

Why machine learning fits

  • The relationship — a stock's returns vs. the market's — is approximately linear over a given window.
  • The data is already structured and numeric: just two columns of returns, nothing to extract from raw input.
  • Interpretability is essential. A regression coefficient tells a client exactly what beta means and how it was calculated.
  • It recalculates quickly and cheaply as new price data arrives — vital in a fast-moving market.
×

Why deep learning doesn't

  • Beta needs only a couple of years of data — nowhere near enough to train a neural net without overfitting to market noise.
  • It would turn beta into even more of a black box — a real liability when decisions must be justified to clients, compliance, and regulators.
  • Since the relationship really is near-linear, a neural net just adds cost and complexity for no better result.
Case 02 · Deep Learning

Automated Document Data Extraction

Method — Intelligent Document Processing (CNN + NLP)
The real-world application

Mobile check deposit is the everyday example — you photograph a check and the app reads and enters the dollar amount, routing number, and account number without you typing anything. The same technology auto-populates your details when you upload a resume to a job application, or scan a driver's license during account signup. This is Intelligent Document Processing, and it's built on deep learning rather than traditional rule-based scanning.

Mobile check depositResume parsingID / license scanning
✓

Why deep learning fits

  • The input is raw, unstructured image data with enormous variation — messy cursive vs. clean type, one-column vs. two-column layouts, state-by-state ID formats.
  • There's no realistic way to hand-code rules for every version, so a CNN recognizes characters and shapes directly from the pixels.
  • Paired with an NLP model that understands context — it knows digits next to a dollar sign are the payment amount, not just any number on the page.
  • It's a textbook fit: high variability, unstructured input, and plenty of scanned data to train on.
×

Why machine learning doesn't

  • Algorithms like logistic regression or decision trees need features hand-selected in advance — impossible to define for every font, handwriting style, and layout.
  • A rules-based system built for one check or resume format breaks the moment it meets a format it hasn't seen.
  • That brittleness is disqualifying for a real product that has to work across thousands of unseen documents — which is exactly why the field moved to deep learning.
The verdict
The right approach comes down to matching the tool to the shape of the problem — not defaulting to whichever method sounds more advanced.
Conclusion

Same logic, two worlds

Looking at both examples together, the same underlying logic applies in finance and in document processing. Stock beta estimation fits machine learning because the data is already structured, the relationship is genuinely close to linear, and interpretability is essential for financial decisions.

Automated document extraction fits deep learning because the input is raw and highly variable, and no reasonable set of hand-coded rules could keep up with every format a resume, ID, or check might take.

In both cases, the right approach comes down to matching the tool to the shape of the problem rather than defaulting to whichever method sounds more advanced.
